As always Hobsabwmn is his own best reviewer in his introduction.
What we have here feels very different from the "Long 19th Trilogy".
There's a pace, here as we clip through a 20th centry that feels short only in the telling, not in the volume of events.

He's clear about why, describing his knowledge as "casual and patchy", resting on "curiously uneven foundations":

> Most of what I have written in this book, except obvious personal judgments of the author, readers will have to take on trust.

He's clear about what we're getting into here: 

> For anyone of my age-group who has lived through all or most of the Short Twentieth Century this is inevitably also an autobiographical endeavour... We are part of this century. It is part of us.

He's honest about this:

> nobody can write about his or her lifetime as one can (and must) write about a period known only from outside

Instead he has:

> accumulated views and prejudices about it as a contemporary rather than as a scholar

This isn't one of his professional reflections:

> My period'... is the nineteenth century

The book makes good on these promises, we get a stimulating and thought provoking account of nearly 100 years.
However it feels more like having Hobsbawn over for dinner, than meeting him at work.

We get a historical sandwich here.
A disaster in the "Age of Catastrophe", with the weight of deaths of the megadeaths that mark this as the most murderous century in history.

Then "the Golden Age", a brief window of progressive advance.

Before the decomposition into neoliberal capital and our present tense.

We end in a present tense that is dismal, but only as far as the 90s.
I think his description of the state as newly indespensible is correct.
This continued to be true even in the waves of austerity that would come later.
Indeed it seems to define many contemporary issues of the 20s today.
Paying for the state brings a groan, but there is little constituency to see it retreat.

Democracy is left in trouble in the 90s, caught between voters who oppose measures which hurt them personally. The solution appears to be a thinning of democractic influence.
Central banks are removed from public control via "independence".
Free to ignore a democractic mandate about how finance may shape the country.
Quangos and arms length bodies and shielded from democratic control and allowed to do public works minimising interference.
Some turn to the legislature or present a fait acompli of "no alternatives".
Others resolve things in back room deals.

Democractic legitimacy is thinned, resting about little "except in the most indirect sense... had themselves, at one time, been elected". An irony given this arrives so shortly after a _democratic triumph_ of the end of the cold war.

I found the book stimulating, if somewhat less of a historical flourish than the other three. Not long after finishing it, I heard [this conversation between Cameron Abadi and Adam Tooze](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DoQgoV-T8wM) on Hobsbawm's legacy.

If we take writers of the left seriously on their terms here, a gap quickly emerges. The coherence of politics in the "long 19th" driven by productive forces, breaks down in the 20th, when sheer violence becomes the driving force.

The critique here would be that the gear change is left unaddressed, and not fully theorised.
Which is a problem, because this is a group that believes the social coordinates are scientifically describable.
That politics can have laws that describe motion as accurately as physics.
Even if we may not be able to predict all the swirls of fluid dynamics on a pond surface, we know something of the underlying theory that helps us describe tangible trends.

So for theory to break down in such a radical way here should be big.
The October revolution appears to occur in all the wrong countries.
Actually existing socialism is drives violence within.
At the height of capitalism by the 90s, mass democracy recedes.

These are remarked upon here but without much reference to the theoretical gulf.
That work appears to be left to other books, and leaves me struggling to patch the messages here into a larger picture.
