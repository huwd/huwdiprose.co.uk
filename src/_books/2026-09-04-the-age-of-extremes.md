As always Hobsbawm is his own best reviewer in his introduction.
What we have here feels very different from the "Long 19th Trilogy".
There's a pace here as we clip through a 20th century that feels short only in the telling, not in the volume of events.

He's clear about why, describing his knowledge as "casual and patchy", resting on "curiously uneven foundations":

> Most of what I have written in this book, except obvious personal judgments of the author, readers will have to take on trust.

He's clear about what we're getting into: 

> For anyone of my age-group who has lived through all or most of the Short Twentieth Century this is inevitably also an autobiographical endeavour... We are part of this century. It is part of us.

and

> nobody can write about his or her lifetime as one can (and must) write about a period known only from outside

instead we have:

> accumulated views and prejudices about it as a contemporary rather than as a scholar

the outside of professional reflections can be found elsewhere in history:

> 'My period', as they say in the trade, is the nineteenth century.

The book makes good on these promises; we get a stimulating and thought provoking account of the short twentieth century.
However perhaps more like having Hobsbawm as a fascinating dinner guest, rather than meeting him at work.

The century is a historical sandwich.
A disaster in the "Age of Catastrophe", with the weight of the megadeaths marking it as the most murderous century on record.

Then "the Golden Age", a brief window of progressive advance.

Before the decomposition into neoliberal capital and our present tense.
The end seems depressed in the loss of the golden era, totally unaware of the period that would follow the 90s.

There is continuity though, he describes a state that has become indispensable, and I believe still is seen that way.

Later waves of austerity, and claims to cut the state seem to continuously flounder on the thin mandate for withdrawing state goods. Even if there's a total lack of consensus around how to pay for them.

We leave democracy in the 90s on the rocks, even in its moment of triumph with the demise of the Soviet Union.

The expression of voters who oppose any measure which hurts them personally and the feeling of things which "must be done", generates an impasse.
The solution is a gradual and plausibly deniable thinning of democratic influence.

In some countries, central banks achieve _independence_ removed from the grasp of both politicians and an electoral mandate.
Finance and its shaping role on a country is given a conventional wisdom of sitting best outside democracy.

Quangos multiplied, answering to no electorate, as arm's length bodies spin off to protect their duties from democratic meddling.

Others pursue their aims in the legislature, deciding via courts and judges.
Some adopt the political language of fait accompli, of "no alternative".
In the US Hobsbawm describes the rise of back room deals.

These themes I think have been a continuous aspect of my lifetime.
A strangely depoliticised and managerially focused period that I think is fleshed out a lot more in Dan Davies's The Unaccountability Machine or even Mark Fisher's [Capitalist Realism](/review/book/2025/12/27/capitalist-realism/).

Many of these cries were echoed in the Brexit era and continue in the feeling of a _stitch-up_. A will denied.

There may be many good reasons for this: are the complexities of Natural England or the Competition and Markets Authority best decided by direct democracy?

In any case though, it's hard not to agree that the 90s starts a period of democratic thinning that continues until today.

Without a mass of movements, with increasingly distant and obscure governance, what's left rests on little

> "except in the most indirect sense... had themselves, at one time, been elected"

I found the book stimulating, if somewhat less of a historical flourish than the other three. Not long after finishing it, I heard [this conversation between Cameron Abadi and Adam Tooze](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DoQgoV-T8wM) on Hobsbawm's legacy.

Tooze observes that the engine of the book's history is driven by violence rather than productive forces. If we take writers of the left seriously on their terms here, a gap quickly emerges. The coherence of politics in the "long 19th", driven by productive forces, breaks down in the 20th, when sheer violence becomes the driving force.

The critique here would be that the gear change is left unaddressed, and not fully theorised.
Which is a problem, because histories informed by Marx and Lenin come from groups that believe the social coordinates can be described with a scientific precision.

Political and historic laws, as strong as physics.
Even if the ripples on a pond's surface defy prediction or even description, their motion is governed by laws.
What we know of the underlying theory helps us describe tangible trends.

So for theory to break down in such a radical way here should be big enough to be central, personal reflection or otherwise.
Socialist revolutions appear to occur in all the wrong countries.
Actually existing socialism with violence driven inward.
At the height of capitalism by the 90s, mass democracy recedes.

These are remarked upon here but without much reference to the theoretical gulf.
That work appears to be left to other books, and leaves me struggling to patch the messages here into a larger picture.
