Another explosive introduction heralds this book, a cavalcade of ideas that burst off the page in dense and lively prose.

We find this is the era where "Capitalism" emerged as a word, and indeed is the subject of its most famous criticism in *Das Kapital*.
The previous revolutionary era's dual revolution splits, "Political revolution retreated, industrial revolution advanced".
1848 as "the first and last European revolution in the (almost) literal sense".
A bourgeois age in an era of aristocratic leadership, surrounded by agents of progress:

> sober men in sober clothes, spreading respectability and a sentiment of racial superiority together with gasworks, railway lines and loans

An era with victims and victors, building towards 1873 with "the Victorian equivalent of the Wall Street Crash of 1929".

Bang.

Hobsbawm thrusts us into a world I never knew could contain so much drama.
Yet already a difference emerges; compared to the previous volume there's a strange mix of enthusiasm about his subject here:

> The author of this book cannot conceal a certain distaste, perhaps a certain contempt, for the age with which it deals, though one mitigated by admiration for its titanic material achievements and by the effort to understand even what he does not like.

We're given a chronicle here of a flourishing point for a liberal capitalism with consequences for politics, history, art and science that continue to the present.

Yet the book clearly establishes itself as not among the era's cheerleaders.

These "titanic material achievements", elevated a class it is easy to disdain as they grew into an unequal world.

The biggest gift of this book is how it fills in a gap I've long struggled to wrap my head around.

The term bourgeois, I've long heard used, often dripping with disdain but applied broadly and in a way that's difficult to pin down.

Is it just being middle class?
Or also something uppity, is it a mindset?
Are they still with us?
Is "bobo", the French bourgeois-bohemian, just a way of showing disdain for the drinkers of fancy espresso and vegan lunch?

This book properly founds the term in a historical era, as a phenomenon and force that renders it transparent.

They did not begin their journey here, rising first during the _age of revolution_. This is the period when bourgeois society "reached its apogee".

A group who "believed in the career open to enterprise and talent, and that their own lives proved its merits", and "Since success was due to personal merit, failure was clearly due to personal lack of merit."

They strode forth with meritocratic zeal and self-congratulation, in an era of spreading technical expertise. As "the educational system became increasingly crucial to industrial development" giving a sense of propulsion forward through the sciences.

Crucially though Hobsbawm notes that this is more meritocratic myth than reality. In south-western Germany, for instance, many of the founders were not "technically innovating craftsmen-entrepreneurs" but came from families with long experience in business, some "grown up in the finance of small princelings".

> The careers of the bourgeois world were indeed open to talent, but the family with a modest amount of education, property and social connections among others of the middle rank undoubtedly started with a relatively enormous advantage…

Brains and merit were not the point. "It was evident that not much in the way of brains was needed for success in business". In Britain and the US, "the triumphs of business were pre-eminently those of poorly educated men using empiricism and common sense".

In 1867, Rhineland industrialists declined to fund the Bonn University anniversary because they had neither themselves enjoyed a university education "nor hitherto given it to their sons".

These facts may come to explain the dripping disdain that now sits on this word, a hypocrisy of meritocratic ideology wrapped in family privilege.

Yet the privilege was deployed to great effect, this class were not merely wealthy but defined by their capital.

> the quintessential bourgeois was a 'capitalist' (i.e. either the possessor of capital, or the receiver of an income derived from such a source, or a profit-making entrepreneur, or all of these things).

It was a class of businessmen, property owners, liberal professions and those at the very top of administration. More than well off:

> The independent artisan and small shopkeeper clearly belonged to a lower middle class or Mittelstand which had little in common with the bourgeoisie except aspiration to its social status. The rich peasant was not a bourgeois, and neither was the white-collar employee.

What distinguished the bourgeois was their faith in a new, shaping worldview:

> They believed in capitalism, in competitive private enterprise, technology, science and reason. They believed in progress, in a certain amount of representative government, a certain amount of civil rights and liberties, so long as these were compatible with the rule of law and with the kind of order which kept the poor in their place. They believed in culture rather than religion…

A group concerned with distinction and respectability, and accused of hypocrisy. The centrality of the networks of family, trust and wealth made them more than their personal distinctions.

Yet there is something of a sea-change here, none the less.
Previous aristocratic rulers founded their power in traditional birth and status.
They counted by dint of their ancestry.

The bourgeoisie broke free of aristocratic birthright, but rebuilt inheritance through a present tense of mobilised capital and familial networks. Yet lensed through the sense of an individual who now counted on their own terms through individual effort, and so became worthy of familial investment.

There was some mobility here, and it congratulated itself on having earned its own power and influence:

> the main characteristic of the bourgeoisie as a class was that it was a body of persons of power and influence, independent of the power and influence of traditional birth and status. To belong to it a man had to be 'someone'…

> More than anything else, it meant superiority. The bourgeois was not merely independent, a man to whom no one (save the state or God) gave orders, but one who gave orders himself. He was not merely an employer, entrepreneur or capitalist but socially a 'master', a 'lord'…

So it was something much more than middle class.

Their "dominance implies inferiority". With those beneath them the main challenge was to try and sort this "subaltern mass" into those who might be raised into respectable lower middle class status and "those who were beyond redemption".

There was a different view looking upwards and sideways however.
Here the result was to both absorb and shape the tastes of the aristocratic.
Having overturned the old order's economic dominance, their imagination only extended to imitating it, with castles and country houses in the aristocratic style.

The chronicle of this high point defines the bourgeoisie as a real historic force, one that certainly still exists today as those of independent means. Though perhaps without the same striking novelty of meteoric rise.

That this was a broadening of societal opportunities is clear. I am inclined to agree that the achievements of its era are impressive because the beneficiaries are so much broader than the monarchic clients of only a few generations earlier.

Yet it stops short.
There is no attempt at universalism here.
It is a jump of a few to the top, that divided the working and middle classes only into different qualities of servant.
A class of masters required those to be mastered.

For all their "[dedication] to… equality of rights and opportunities and freedom", they were happy to ensure that it stayed in a purely public sphere for competition.
These same arguments were never to be extended as individual rights to dissolve the patriarchal family, instead "the classic phase of bourgeois society reinforced and exaggerated it."

> The crucial point is that the structure of the bourgeois family flatly contradicted that of bourgeois society.

Perhaps this inequality was a feature, not a bug.
With superiority so valued, yet "not based on collective, institutionalized, traditional inequalities, dependence had to be an individual relationship", and "because superiority was so uncertain for the individual, it had to have one form that was permanent and secure."

And so the society of individuals coalesced into new dynasties.

The story and period fills a deep gap for me and I find myself with a new language.
I have an intuition that whilst much of the raw reality of the period is not a direct contemporary analogy, shockwaves of this era still shape the contours of British class and society today.

An era of great leaps, changes of elites and a burgeoning capital class who revolted against what came before, but would have wished to have stopped what came after.
For it is followed by the mass.
