Another explosive introduction heralds this book, a cavalcade of ideas that burst off the page in dense and lively prose.

We find this is the era where "Capitalism" emerged as a word, and indeed is the subject of it's most famous critism in Das Kapital.
The previous revolutionary era's dual revoltion splits, "Political revolution retreated, industrial revolution advanced".
1848 as "the first and last European revolution in the (almost) literal sense".
A bourgeois age in an era of aristocratic leadership, surrounded by agents of progress:

> sober men in sober clothes, spreading respectability and a sentiment of racial superiority together with gasworks, railway lines and loans

An era with victims and victors, building towards 1872 with "the Victorian equivalent of the Wall Street Crash of 1929".

Bang.

Hobsbawm thrusts us into a world I never knew could contain so much drama.
Yet already a difference emerges, compared to his first book there's a strange mix of enthusiasm about his subject here:

> The author of this book cannot conceal a certain distaste, perhaps a certain contempt, for the age with which it deals, though one mitigated by admiration for its titanic material achievements and by the effort to understand even what he does not like.

We're given a chronical here of a flourishing point for a liberal capitalism with consequence for politics, history art and science that continue to the present.

Yet the book clearly establishes itself as not among the eras cheerleaders.

These "titanic material achivements", elevated a class it is easy to distain as they grew into an unequal world.

The biggest gift of this book is how it fills in a gap I've long struggled to wrap my head around.

The term Bourgeoise, I've long heard used, often dripping with distain but applied broadly and in a way that's difficult to pin down.

Is it just being middle class?
Or also something uppity, is it a mindset?
Are they still with us?
When bohemian, and French in Bobo, is it just a way of showing distain for the drinkers of fancy expresso and vegan lunch?

This book properly founds the term in a historical era, as a phenomenan and force that renders it transparent.

They did not begin their journey here, rising first during the _age of revolution_ and "which reached its apogee at this period".

A group who "believed in the career open to enterprise and talent, and that their own lives proved its merits", and "Since success was due to personal merit, failure was clearly due to personal lack of merit."

They strode forth with metirtocratic zeal and self congradulation, in an era of spreading technical expertise. As "the educational system became increasingly crucial to industrial development" giving a sense of propulsion forward through the sciences.

Crucially though Hobsawmn notes that this is more meritocratic myth than reality. The firms that rose to this pinnicle were not founded on technical knowhow, but were based were those who crew on the finance of small princelings. 

> The careers of the bourgeois world were indeed open to talent, but the family with a modest amount of education, property and social connections among others of the middle rank undoubtedly started with a relatively enormous advantage.

Brains and merit were not the point. "It was evident that not much in the way of brains was needed for success in business". In Britain and the US, "the triumphs of business were pre-eminently those of poorly educated men using empiricism and common sense". Indeed many of the industrialists declined to fund universities as they'd neither needed it, nor saw it of use to their sons.

In 1867, Rhineland industrialists declined to fund the Bonn University anniversary because they had neither themselves enjoyed a university education "nor hitherto given it to their sons".

These facts may come to explain the dripping distain that now sits on this word, a hypocracy of meritocratic ideology wrapped in family privildige.

Yet the privildige was deployed to great effect, this class were not merely wealthy but defined by their capital.

> the quintessential bourgeois was a 'capitalist' (i.e. either the possessor of capital, or the receiver of an income derived from such a source, or a profit-making entrepreneur, or all of these things).

It was a class of businessmen, property owners, liberal professions and those at the very top of administration. More than well off:

> The independent artisan and small shopkeeper clearly belonged to a lower middle class or Mittelstand which had little in common with the bourgeoisie except aspiration to its social status. The rich peasant was not a bourgeois, and neither was the white-collar employee.

There was a distinction here, some of which was shaped by a worldview not shared by those shopkeeping or peasant peers in wealth:

>They believed in capitalism, in competitive private enterprise, technology, science and reason. They believed in progress, in a certain amount of representative government, a certain amount of civil rights and liberties, so long as these were compatible with the rule of law and with the kind of order which kept the poor in their place. They believed in culture rather than religion…

A group concerned with distinction, respectaility and dripping with hypocrisy. The centrality of the networks of family, trust and wealth made them more than their personal distinctions.

Yet there is something of a sea-change here, none the less.
Previous aristocratic rulers founded their power in birthright, title, legal privilidge and ascribed rank.
They counted by dint of their ancestry.

The bourgeoisie broke free of aristocratic birthright, but rebuilt inheritance through a present tense of mobilised capital and familial networks. Yet lensed through the sense of an individual who now counted on their own terms through individual effort, and so became worthy of familial investment.

There was some mobility here, and it congratulated itself on having earned it's own power and influence:

> the main characteristic of the bourgeoisie as a class was that it was a body of persons of power and influence, independent of the power and influence of traditional birth and status. To belong to it a man had to be 'someone'.

> More than anything else, it meant superiority. The bourgeois was not merely independent, a man to whom no one (save the state or God) gave orders, but one who gave orders himself. He was not merely an employer, entrepreneur or capitalist but socially a 'master', a 'lord'…

So it was something much more than middle class, and indeed looked down on the merely middle class who could not wield the heat of capital:

> Sociologically the difference between working and middle classes was that between servant-keepers and potential servants.

Their dominance implied an inferiority of others, but own those of lower status.

The class seemed to mingle, both absorb and shape the tastes of the aristocratic.
Having achieved dominance in breaking hundreds of years of fuedal economic rule, their imagiation only extended to inhabiting palaces of similar or greater gradure.

The chronicle of this high point definse the boureoise as a real historic force, one that certainly still exists today as those of "independent means". Though perhapse without the same striking novelty of meteroic rise.

That this was a broadening of societal opportunities is clear. I am inclined to agree that the achivements of it's era are impressive because the beneficaries are so much broader than the monarchic clients of only a few generations earlier.

Yet it stops short.
There is no attempt at universalism here.
It is a jump of a few to the top, that divided the working and middle classes only into different qualities of servant.
A class of masters required those to be mastered.

For all their "[dedication] to… equality of rights and opportunities and freedom", they were happy to ensure that stayed in a purely public sphere for competition.
These same arguments were never to be extended as individual rigihts to dissolve the patriarchal family, instead "the classic phase of bourgeois society reinforced and exaggerated it."                     

> The crucial point is that the structure of the bourgeois family flatly contradicted that of bourgeois society.                        

This inequality was a feature not a bug.
With superiority so valued, yet "not based on collective, institutionalized, traditional inequalities, dependence had to be an individual relationship", and "because superiority was so uncertain for the individual, it had to have one form that was permanent and secure."

And so the soceity of indiviudals coalellsed into new dynasties.

The story and period fills a deep gap for me and I find myself with a new langauge.
I have an intuition that whilst much of the raw reality of the period is not a direct contemproary analogy, shockwaves of this era still shape the contours of British class and society today.

An era of great leaps, changes of elites and a burgeoning capital class who revolted against what came before, but would have wished to have stopped what came after.
For it is followed by the mass.
