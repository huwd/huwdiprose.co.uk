A fascinating chaser to [Hyperpolitics](/review/book/2026/09/04/hyperpolitics/).

There we walk among the ruins of past political institutions and lament the way modern movements for change can't stick the landing.

Here we speak to a practitioner whose career spans the sweep of this change.

There are eerie resonances too.

On the demise of a sense of collective struggle amid atomisation, even among the left's own identity politics:

> Not so much intersectionality of identities, but intersectionality of struggles

An insistance that the will and power here is founded in grouping, more than ourselves:

> It is in collectivities that we find reservoirs of hope and optimism

with the result:

> It's trying. Trying and trying again. Never stopping. That is a victory in itself

In many ways the thesis seems to agree with the hyperpolitics thesis of contemporary atomisation undermining pressure for change.

However there's perhaps more optimism here.
I can't help but feel that here we have a woman who as faced a share of _struggle_ that would have melted all but the most resilient.
Yet speaks lovingly of comrades and fellows along the way.
She must have found communities to sustain her, and that must underpin the solidarity she shows to others in these lectures.

Her advice to contemporary activists seems to be to try and find ways to reverse or transcend the hyperpolitics curse.
And not to be waylaid by other promises,

Of the political _savior_ we may vote for:

> What we have lacked over these last five years is not the right president, but rather well-organized mass movements.

to just reform movements by their long run outcomes:

> So prison reform has always only created better prisons.

and a feeling that a technical fix can supplant political success:

> The problem is that it is often assumed that the eradication of the legal apparatus is equivalent to the abolition of racism.

Instead she would centre coming together, solidarity in common struggles and a return to something that sounds like the mass politics of the mid 20th.

> We cannot go on as usual. We cannot pivot the center. We cannot be moderate.

She speaks well and perusaively, with a clarity of thinking developed over a long career.

That was enough to underpin my enjoyment after a raft of heavier technical texts.

But I'm not sure there's a roadmap for what's next to be found here,
Just the comfort against dispair from someone who should know the value of that better than most.
