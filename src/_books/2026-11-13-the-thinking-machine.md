This isn't a bad book, Indeed it contains useful insights to our current world. However it's motivating cause I think will age poorly, and it's approach to the central character of Huang sits poorly with me.

It's trace of Nvidia's rise to power mirrors other books I've read in recent years. However it feels fresh in starting before our era of LLM fueled hyperbole. There is something tangible and compelling to me in the telling. How Huang worked through the early computer graphics industry I was around to see the fruits of. Early games, Jurassic park's CGI, the hunger of John Carmack for more complete virtual worlds.

I was fascinated by the idea that Huang is among the last of a generation who artisanally hand planned chip transitor gates; before software took over the placement and the role moved up to planning more abstract architectures. A fate awaiting other technologists today?

Business sections also popped off the page, the dynamics of intel during the height of Moore's law, why do anything fancy with compute when you can just wait two years for the existing architecture to double in speed. Huang's losers bet on parallel computing, almost to my eye out of a contrarian 
belief, not foresight.

Nvidia accompanied by youth in advertising spreads in games magazines. Apparently solely because our desire for more highly rendered worlds was the only viable consumer demand that outstripped Moore's law. Nvidia used gaming as a refuge for it's technical choices.

By the time AI comes on the scene as the true bonanza of compute demand Nvidia had been waiting for, the cast of characters has grown broad. Many I have encountered in other books.

Yet what this book does well is call out the sheer unlikeliness of what has unfolded. Much like parallel computing, Neural networks seem to be a dismissed losers bet. The idea they would come together into something explosive was absurd until it was not.

One thing that tempered my admiration here, restrains me from calling these people visionaries, is that it seems unlikely they knew the impact themselves. Tremendous luck intervened. I encounter true believers of particular technologies all the time, nerds love their toys. Nerds are irate that others can't see their innate superiority, nerds make promises about the future of only you'd hand it to them. Nerds are wrong all the time about this.

These people don't seem that special to me, even Huang. They are just the victors of good, smart calls vaulted to the heavens by events entirely beyond their control, prediction and perhaps comprehension at the time.

Yet I could not love this book. For all the skepticism at the end the author cannot seem but to admire Huang, and for all the wrong reasons. I suspect that compared to other barons, Huang is a nicer one. I might even enjoy his company where I doubt Zuckerberg and I would share an amicable meal. Yet this book wants to labour the humble origins, spin a meritocratic web that propelled the man. It wants us to see how hard he worked, his "superior intellect", at times it flirts with 996 work cultures and an always on approach. Huang works every weekend but then sheepishly admits he missed his children's youth. Pays, a now hollow seeming, genuflection to his wife's retreat from a promising career to raise their children, so he didn't have to.

Perhaps worse it constantly brings up Huang's management style of shouting at employees, from the earliest days. It stalwartly attempts to downplay this, explain it away.

The world it presents us is one of cut throat dog-eat-dog competition, where only exemplary and superhuman work ethic keeps a company in business. Indeed I believe that microchips were genuinely competitive in silicon valley across the period. A start up could disrupt.

However this book wants us to believe that necessitates a business culture. One in which an enormously centralised dictatorial control is both essential and admirable to getting a corps of people to march in step. One where a little shouting is forgivable, if it helps troops march to a common goal and their ultimate profit. Especially if you sheepishly admit you're sorry, make nice, and it was all for common good afterwards.

I doubt this. Though it's a well founded myth.

Huang's lack of delegation makes me wonder what Nvidia left on the table because the demands of hundreds of people and the their comms overheads could not be parsed by one man, even if he is dip sampling their week notes.

What would have been achieved in a more empowering culture, or one that relied less on a dictatorial control in exchanged for more downward trust. How much was lost because of the time it took for Huang to "see", because his sight was all that mattered?

In any case this seems moot now. In the American dream of performance legitimacy, he made many who worked for him fabulously rich. Everyone has their price, and for generational wealth a boss whose bark is worse than their bite is probably a price few wouldn't  pay.

Yet it's hard to see the author as someone who doesn't hold some admiration for all of this. His big falling out with Huang isn't about any of this. Instead it transpired he's worried AI will mean no-one buys his books (... Is suspect the opposite turns out to be true).

I'm with Huang in my disappointment of the author. "Oh, so that's what all this was about, how boring". We've not picked a fight over myriad other things that should have vexed Huang.

Instead we've spun a "great man of digital history" thesis justified by the attempt to be a prescient Cassandra of AI doom. I find both propositions tedious.

On top of that I read this in November 2026, soon after Huang's fall from grace. It is hard to square the description of an honest man, motivated by doing right by his family, with his actions. Beyond that I don't care to speculate much. One thing I often think as newspapers tear scraps off someone for infidelities, is how little attention is paid to what their family must feel in these times. Anger? Shame? Vindication? Who knows, but few seem to care. I might wish folks take their pound of flesh and the misdeeds of a partner don't become everything everyone knows about me forever.

I think at the root of my disquiet about this book is actually another thing. We watch these men, these losers frankly. Though a term I use with an edge of endearment. People who probably should have ended up like tech folk I know, handsomely middle class or above, well compensated, boring everyone at every talk about their favourite tech they've gone all in on.

People who tinkered and tampered.

The person who comes to mind for me as my aspirational exemplar here is Aaron Swartz. Someone who had the good sense to do much of this for general intellectual curiousity or a perceived public benefit. Someone who did not decide to leverage this into fabulous personal wealth.

It's gauling to me to see these men IPO into multi billionaires pivoted through more audacious and egregious thefts than Aaron ever attempted. That the sellers of silicone spades to fuel this have become the most profitable companies in the world off its back. Because of a long shot with CUDA. Genius only in a lucky hindsight. The point is not against these longshots or tinkers, I'd have more. Rather the idea that they were always on a path toward this. These are evolutionary accidents, not superior beings.

I can't forgive this book for not taking a more systemic and less biographical approach to the defining questions of our era. It's hesitant love for Huang, and forgiveness or things I'd be horrified to see at work. I don't forgive it for not making more of the hypocrisy of those who rode it's profits from competition to monopoly rent, from employees to investors and state economies.

I feel annoyed my indignation about the variable application of law doesn't shine through books like these.

Because they didn't make themselves great, a system around them did. How that is enabled or inhibited appears to be less a function of liberal law than the elite fears of the day. Fears which move across short years to contradictory outcome.

Because Aaron Swartz is dead, and the protagonists of these books are rich.
